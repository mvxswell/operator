What to Do When You Lose Your Biggest Customer

Protect cash and delivery in the first two days, then find out why they left. Do not fill the hole with a discount that buys worse work. Rebuild so one account cannot sink you again.

The first 48 hours are about cash, not a new logo

A big cancellation feels like a sales emergency. It is usually a cash and focus emergency first. Before you start calling everyone you know, write three numbers.

How much monthly or quarterly cash just disappeared.

What costs were tied only to that account.

How many weeks of runway you have if nothing else changes.

Cut or pause the costs that existed only to serve that customer — extra hours, a dedicated contractor, inventory you will not need, a tool you added for them. Do not cut the work that still creates other sales, and do not cut people in a panic on day one. You need a clean picture before you make a permanent change.

Tell the team what you know and what you do not. Silence fills with rumors. A short, honest note — "we lost the account, here is what it does and does not mean for this month's work" — is enough. If anyone was assigned only to that account, give them a temporary owner and a temporary job the same day.

If the customer still owes you money, treat collections as a separate, calm process. A lost account can still pay a final invoice. Do not trade the receivable for a vague promise to "revisit in six months."

Find out why they left, with evidence

You cannot fix what you refuse to hear. Ask for a real conversation, not a form. Go in with a short list: Was it price, service, a missed promise, a new internal buyer, or a competitor? When did they start looking? What would have had to be true for them to stay?

Listen for the moment things actually broke. "We're going in a different direction" is often a polite ending, not a cause. The cause is usually earlier: a string of late jobs, a surprise invoice, a sales promise operations could not keep, or a relationship that lived in one person who left.

Write it down in your own words and share it with whoever owns delivery, not just sales. If the reason is operational, a new flyer will not help. If the reason is that you were expensive and also unreliable, discounting makes you cheap and unreliable.

You may not get a candid answer. That is still information. Look at the work: on-time rate, callbacks, scope changes, how long invoices sat, and how often they had to chase you. The file often explains what the goodbye email will not.

Do not buy the next account at any price

The urge is to replace the revenue this week. That is how you sign a bad deal: a steep discount, a custom scope you cannot repeat, or a customer who will consume the same capacity for less money.

Run the next opportunity through a simple screen. Does it pay for the capacity it will use, with margin left over? Can you deliver it without starving better accounts? If you have to say yes to win it, what are you saying no to? If the only way to land it is a price you would regret on a normal Tuesday, wait.

A temporary revenue dip is painful. A year of work that does not cover its cost is worse, because it looks like a recovery in the top line and a leak in cash.

If you do need short-term volume, prefer work you already know how to do, at a price that still contributes, even if it is not as large as the account you lost. Existing customers you have under-served are often a better first call than a brand-new logo.

Talk to the accounts you still have

While one door is closing, the rest of the book needs attention. A big departure can rattle other customers if they hear it first from the market. Call the next few important accounts. You do not have to announce the loss in detail. You do have to be present, confirm upcoming work, and ask if anything is getting in their way.

Look at concentration while you do it. Suppose, as a hypothetical example and not a statistic, that one customer was 30 or 40 percent of revenue. That kind of concentration was a risk even when they were happy. You do not fix concentration in a week, but you can stop adding to it. Set a simple rule for the next two quarters: no new account should be allowed to become that large until the rest of the pipeline is deeper.

That rule will feel slow when you are scared. It is how you avoid repeating the same week a year from now.

Rebuild the pipeline so this is not the whole business

Once cash is stable enough to think, split the recovery into two jobs: demand and delivery.

On demand, be precise. If they left because you were late, new leads will only create more late work. Fix the delivery promise first, then spend on acquisition. If they left because you had no relationship below one champion, the fix is coverage — more than one contact, a regular review, a clear owner — not a bigger ad budget. If they left because a competitor offered a tighter package, name what you will and will not match.

On delivery, remove the special exceptions that existed only for the big account if those exceptions were stressing the rest of the work. Some of them were worth it. Some of them were how the rest of the company got worse while one logo was flattered.

Give the rebuild a review date, not a vibe. In 30 days you should know what cash looks like, which conversations happened, and which experiments you started. In 90 days you should know whether the pipeline is actually broader, or whether you just got busy.

A worked example

Suppose a 20-person service firm has $200,000 of typical monthly revenue, and one customer has been $70,000 of it. These are example figures, so you can see the shape — not a claim about your book. That customer cancels with 30 days left.

Cash first: $70,000 will drop out after that month. $12,000 of contractor hours and a rented extra vehicle were only for them and can come off at the end of the period. The remaining gap is still large. The owner has eight weeks of cash if nothing else changes, which is tight but not immediate insolvency.

Why they left: jobs ran late during the busy season, and a new manager at the customer had already been comparing bids. Price was the stated reason. Lateness was the earlier one.

What not to do: take a new account at cost "just to keep the crew busy." What to do: call the next five accounts, finish the remaining work cleanly, collect the last invoices, and bid only on work that still contributes. Over the next quarter, cap any one account's share and put a named owner on each important relationship, so one champion's departure cannot erase the book.
