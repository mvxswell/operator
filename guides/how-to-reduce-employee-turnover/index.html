How to Reduce Employee Turnover

Turnover is usually a lagging report of a job that stopped making sense. Find why people actually leave, fix the work and the load, and talk with your keepers before a resignation is already typed.

Start with why they left, not with a new perk

This page is the retention system for the whole team: why people leave, how the job is built, and the conversations you have before anyone resigns. When one key person has already quit, use What to Do When Your Best Employee Quits for that week, then come back here so the next one is less of a surprise.

A raise, a Friday lunch, or a new title can help a healthy job. They rarely save a job that is confusing, unfair, or stuck. If the last three people who left had the same complaint — they could not get a decision, they were covering two roles, or they had no path — a bonus will not fix it. You will just pay more for the same problem.

Write down the last several departures. For each one, note tenure, role, whether you were surprised, and the reason they gave. Then add the reason you believe, if it is different. You are looking for a pattern, not a personality. One person leaving for a spouse's move is life. Three coordinators leaving in a year because every exception still comes to you is a system.

If you do not do exit conversations, start. Keep them short and specific. Ask what would have made the job worth staying in, and what they had to work around every week. Do not argue. You are collecting a pattern across departures. The handover for one resignation is a different conversation.

Look at the job, not the person

Most people do not leave a company in the abstract. They leave a load, a manager, or a lack of progress. Walk the role the way you would walk a production line.

Is the work clear? Can someone say what a good week looks like without asking you? If "it depends" is the only standard, they are guessing, and guessing is exhausting.

Is the load fair and finite? A role that regularly needs nights and weekends, or that absorbs every gap when someone else is out, will turn over. The people who stay are not more loyal. They have not found the door yet.

Can they see a future? That does not always mean a promotion. It can mean a harder craft, a clearer path to lead a small crew, or the chance to own a process. If the only growth is "do more of the same, faster," your best people will outgrow you first.

Who do they report to, in practice? If you hired a manager and still make every call, the manager cannot coach and the team cannot trust the manager. That is a delegation problem wearing a turnover costume. The delegation guide is the longer version of that problem.

Fix the work before you recruit again

Once you can name the pattern, change the job before you post it. Hiring into an unfixed role is how you buy another resignation.

Typical fixes, in the order you should try them:

  1. Write the real job. One page is enough: the outcomes they own, the decisions they can make alone, and the decisions that still need you. Share it with the people in the role and ask what you missed.
  2. Cut or move the work that does not belong there. If your lead installer is also quoting, chasing invoices, and covering the parts run, you do not have a lead installer. You have a pile. Move or drop something before you ask them to stay.
  3. Set a decision rule so they are not waiting on you. "Refunds under $200, you decide. Over that, text me and I'll answer the same day." A rule is kinder than an open door you never walk through.
  4. Make the load visible. A simple weekly count — jobs finished, hours, callbacks, open issues — shows whether one person is quietly absorbing the overflow. Fix the overflow. Do not praise the person for drowning politely.
  5. Then talk about pay and title. Pay should be fair for the market and the work. It should not be the first lever, and it should not be how you apologize for a broken job.

If you cannot fix the job, be honest in the next hire. "This role still has a lot of exceptions, and I am the bottleneck on price" is a rough sentence. It is better than a polished posting that loses someone in four months.

Talk with your keepers before they have a speech

Do not wait for the two-week notice. Once a resignation is written, you are usually negotiating an exit, not a stay.

Pick the people you would least want to replace. Once a quarter, have a short stay conversation. It is not a review. Ask what is getting in their way, what they want to be doing more of in six months, and whether anything would make them look around. Then do something with the answer, even if the something is "I can't change that this quarter, and here is why."

A conversation with no follow-through teaches them not to tell you the truth next time.

Do not save pay for the doorway. Counteroffers at the door are a weak habit. A sudden raise tells everyone else that walking to the door is how pay gets fixed. If the job is sound and pay is the gap, close it in the stay conversation, while they are still here. When a resignation is already in hand, the doorway decision belongs in What to Do When Your Best Employee Quits.

Watch leading signs, not just resignations

Resignations are late. Watch the earlier signals.

Requests for schedule flexibility that you keep deferring.

A strong person going quiet in meetings.

A rising error or callback rate.

A manager who has stopped giving real feedback. A skipped one-on-one is not a small thing if it happens for a month.

None of these prove someone is leaving. Together they tell you where to have a conversation this week, not after the email arrives.

When someone still leaves

You will still lose people. The aim is fewer surprises and a business that can absorb a departure.

When a resignation lands, that week is a different job from this system. Protect the work, hand off what only they know, and then decide what happens to the role. What to Do When Your Best Employee Quits is that playbook. Bring what you learn back to the job, for the people who stayed.
